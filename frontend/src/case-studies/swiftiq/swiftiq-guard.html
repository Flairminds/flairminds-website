The Compliance Problem We Solved

Global financial institutions process millions of SWIFT messages daily — payment instructions, letters of credit, trade finance confirmations — each a potential vector for money laundering, sanctions evasion, or trade-based financial crime. Traditional rule-based screening systems are brittle: they catch obvious flags but miss behavioural anomalies, produce excessive false positives, and generate compliance records that read like machine output rather than professional audit trails.

SwiftIQ Guard was engineered to solve all three simultaneously, combining four complementary analysis tiers into a single decision chain that produces a consensus risk verdict any compliance officer can act on — and any regulator can audit.

The 4-Tier Decision Chain

Every SWIFT message passes through four sequential tiers, ordered by speed and certainty. The Safe-Path policy ensures a high-confidence flag from any earlier tier cannot be overridden by a lower-confidence signal from a later one.

1
Deterministic Rule Engine
Instant · Zero Latency

Parsing · Schema Validation · AML Rules

Raw SWIFT text is converted to structured JSON. Mandatory fields (:20:, :32A:, :50:, :59:) are validated. Three AML rule categories execute in sequence: High-Risk Corridor Detection matches countries against a geopolitical risk registry; Structuring Alerts flag amounts within $500 of the $10,000 CTR threshold; TBML Scrutiny analyses MT700 trade finance messages for dual-use HS codes. A CRITICAL flag here activates Safe-Path and determines the final verdict.

2
ML Intelligence
Probabilistic · ~120ms

XGBoost Risk Classification · Isolation Forest Anomaly Detection

Six feature vectors — amount, corridor risk, round-number flag, just-below-threshold flag, counterparty account age, and 7-day transaction frequency — are sent to the Flask microservice. XGBoost produces a four-class risk probability. Isolation Forest flags transactions whose profile deviates from the historical baseline, catching novel structuring patterns invisible to rules.

3
Sanction Screening
Fuzzy Matching · OFAC · EU · UN

Multi-List Watchlist Matching with Levenshtein Distance

Entity names from :50: and :59: fields are screened against OFAC SDN, EU Consolidated, and UN Security Council lists. Exact matching handles canonical names. Levenshtein distance fuzzy matching with an 80% similarity threshold catches transliteration variants and deliberate typos — for example, "TEHERAN IMPORT EXPORT CO" matches the OFAC list at 92% similarity. A confirmed hit escalates the verdict to CRITICAL regardless of Tiers 1 and 2.

4
Consensus and AI Synthesis
Safe-Path Policy · Llama 3.1

Risk Consensus · On-Premise Audit Narrative Generation

The Safe-Path policy combines all upstream signals: the final risk level is the highest severity produced by any tier, with critical flags explicitly overriding lower-severity ML scores. Llama 3.1 then synthesises all flags, probabilities, and override metadata into a 2–4 sentence professional audit narrative, stored in Supabase and rendered in the officer review interface — generated entirely on-premise.

Operational Workflow

A compliance officer submits a SWIFT message — single or batch — through the screening interface. The four-tier pipeline executes and the consensus result with AI narrative is displayed, typically within two to three seconds for single messages. Messages flagged at HIGH or CRITICAL automatically enter the compliance queue. An authorised Officer reviews and takes one of three actions — Clear, Block, or Escalate — with mandatory case notes. Every action is written to the immutable audit log with the officer's user ID, their role, a timestamp, and the full screening snapshot. Viewers can read the audit log but cannot act on cases, ensuring segregation of duties.

Key Engineering Decisions

On-premise LLM is not a compromise — it is a feature.

Running Llama 3.1 locally via Ollama initially felt like a constraint of data privacy requirements. In practice it became a competitive advantage: no external API rate limits, more predictable latency, and audit narrative quality indistinguishable from a frontier API for this constrained, structured task.

The Safe-Path policy eliminates a category of false negatives.

Early versions used a weighted average of tier scores. In testing, a CRITICAL rule flag for TBML dual-use goods was diluted by a LOW ML score, passing the transaction to the officer queue instead of blocking it. The maximum-severity Safe-Path policy eliminated this failure mode entirely.

Fuzzy matching threshold calibration is a business decision, not a technical one.

Setting the Levenshtein threshold too low floods the compliance queue with false positives. Too high and genuine phonetic variants of sanctioned entity names pass undetected. The final 80% threshold was chosen in consultation with compliance requirements, not optimised for F1 score alone.

Conclusion

SwiftIQ Guard demonstrates that enterprise-grade AML compliance technology does not require expensive vendor platforms or external data sharing. A carefully architected open-source stack — React, Flask, XGBoost, Isolation Forest, Supabase, and Llama 3.1 on Ollama — delivers screening quality, audit trail depth, and regulatory defensibility at the level of production financial institution deployment.

The four-tier decision chain with Safe-Path consensus is the core insight: by combining deterministic rules, probabilistic ML, fuzzy entity matching, and AI narrative synthesis with a maximum-severity override policy, the platform achieves both high recall on true positives and a professionally auditable record of every decision.
